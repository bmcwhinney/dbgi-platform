Beneath the Roseau Valley, water heated by the same volcanic system that gives Dominica its hot springs and boiling lake has been circulating for millennia. For the past ten years, a small team of engineers and civil servants has been trying to turn that heat into something the national grid can use.

This spring, it finally worked. The first commercial-scale geothermal plant on the island began delivering power, drawing on wells drilled more than a mile into the mountainside. Officials say the plant will eventually supply a meaningful share of the island's electricity, cutting reliance on imported diesel and stabilising prices that have swung with global fuel markets for decades.

For founders, the bigger story is what a reliable, cheap baseload power source unlocks. Data processing, cold storage, and light manufacturing all become more viable once electricity costs stop being the first line item in every business plan. A handful of early-stage companies are already positioning themselves around the plant, from a proposed agro-processing facility to a small server colocation venture aimed at regional clients.

"The geothermal story was never just about the grid," says one engineer who worked on the original resource assessment. "It was about giving the next generation of businesses a foundation they could actually build on."

Challenges remain. Transmission infrastructure needs upgrading to move power from the interior to population centres, and the utility is still working through the tariff structure that will determine how quickly savings reach ordinary households and businesses. But after ten years of drilling, testing, and false starts, the island has proof that its volcanic geology is an asset rather than just a hazard to plan around.
